# Literature review: retrieval, RAG, and personal-archive research for mailchat-vault (2026-11-13)

Scope: the published research most relevant to mailchat-vault's core problems — BM25/FTS5 ranking, hybrid sparse+dense fusion (RRF), dependency-free hashed embeddings, consent-gated RAG with citations, email threading, content-addressed identity/dedup, and personal-information-management (PIM) re-finding behavior — plus a ranked list of concrete improvement candidates grounded in that literature. Method: every paper below was verified against a primary source (arXiv abstract page fetched, author- or publisher-hosted PDF read, or DOI resolved through doi.org to its publisher record); citation counts come from the Semantic Scholar Graph API (batch endpoint), labeled "Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13". Semantic Scholar does not index the two non-paper primary sources (JWZ's web document, RFC 5256); they are marked "not indexed" rather than given a count. OpenAlex was used only as a bibliographic cross-check to resolve correct DOIs; no OpenAlex counts are reported. Seed-list corrections found during verification are flagged inline (Lewis & Knowles is IP&M 1997, not CHI; FastCDC is ATC '16, not FAST '16; the 2004 Teevan paper's authors are Teevan/Alvarado/Ackerman/Karger).

Implementation facts this review is grounded in (from the repo at review time): FTS5 index currently covers only `body` and `author` columns (`crates/vault-core/src/storage.rs`); hybrid fusion is RRF with k=60, deterministic tie-breaks (`vector.rs:328`); the vector leg is the bundled `hash-ngram-v1` model (512-dim, i8-quantized, ~75 ms linear scan per 100k messages, MIN_COSINE 0.08, first 2,000 chars of body embedded); Ask summarizes the top-8 rows with bodies truncated to 500 chars (`llm.rs`), with a validated-MATCH-translation step and a plain-term-search fallback; threading is one-hop In-Reply-To/References resolution; media is content-addressed by SHA-256; runtime downloads are forbidden (offline promise).

## 1. Classic IR ranking (BM25 and its extensions)

The ranking function inside FTS5 is BM25; this literature says where its remaining headroom is for a field-structured corpus like mail/chat.

- **Robertson & Zaragoza (2009), "The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond", Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval 3(4):333–389.** [DOI 10.1561/1500000019](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019); [author-hosted PDF](https://www.staff.city.ac.uk/~sbrp622/papers/foundations_bm25_review.pdf) (verified). Citations: 7,084 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the canonical monograph deriving BM25 from the probabilistic framework and cataloguing its extensions — term-frequency saturation, length normalization, and especially per-field weighting (BM25F). It argues BM25's few parameters are robust precisely because they are *not* learned per-query, which matches using SQLite's stock bm25() ranking.
  Relevance: reference text for any tuning of FTS5's bm25() parameters (k1, b) and for the field-weighting change recommended below; also justifies keeping a strong un-tuned lexical leg as the fusion baseline.

- **Robertson, Zaragoza & Taylor (2004), "Simple BM25 extension to multiple weighted fields", CIKM 2004.** [DOI 10.1145/1031171.1031181](https://doi.org/10.1145/1031171.1031181). Citations: 853 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: introduces BM25F — instead of concatenating fields, score each field with its own BM25 and combine with per-field weights (e.g., title ≫ body), because term evidence is not equally strong in every field.
  Relevance: the single most directly applicable ranking paper here. `messages_fts` indexes only `body,author` today; a `subject` column with a higher bm25() weight is a small schema migration (FTS5's bm25() auxiliary function already accepts per-column weights) and a plausible quality win, since subject-line terms are the strongest single predictor of email relevance.

- **Rocchio (1971), "Relevance feedback in information retrieval", in The SMART Retrieval System, Prentice-Hall.** No DOI (book chapter). Citations: 3,620 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the classic pseudo-relevance-feedback (PRF) construction — expand the query with terms from the top-ranked results, weighted toward them and away from bottom-ranked documents; simple, unsupervised, and effective when the top of the ranking is mostly relevant.
  Relevance: PRF over FTS5 results can run entirely on-device (SELECT the top 10 results, pick discriminating terms, rewrite the MATCH expression) — a zero-dependency relevance boost for short queries where the vector leg is weak.

## 2. Rank fusion

The implemented fusion leg — score(d) = Σ 1/(60 + rank) — comes directly from this small literature.

- **Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher (2009), "Reciprocal Rank Fusion Outperforms Condorcet and Individual Rank Learning Methods", SIGIR 2009 (poster, 2 pp.).** [DOI 10.1145/1571941.1572114](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114); [author-hosted PDF](https://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf) (verified, incl. the sentence "k = 60 was fixed during a pilot investigation" and that k=60 "was near-optimal"). Citations: 1,651 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: fusing ranked lists with 1/(k+rank), k=60, beats every individual input system and Condorcet Fuse across TREC experiments, and matched CombMNZ — with no training data.
  Relevance: this is the exact algorithm shipped in `rrf_fuse` (k=60, `RRF_K`). The paper's headline — fusion with no calibration — is precisely why it suits an offline app with no click data.

- **Fox & Shaw (1994), "Combination of Multiple Searches", TREC-2 Proceedings, NIST.** No DOI (NIST proceedings); S2 indexes the TREC record. Citations: 1,305 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the TREC-2 combination study that established CombSUM/CombMNZ — normalize per-system scores, then sum (optionally multiplied by the number of lists retrieving the document). CombMNZ was the standard to beat that RRF matched.
  Relevance: the baseline alternative if score normalization ever becomes attractive; also the origin of "documents found by both legs deserve a bonus", which RRF approximates for free.

- **Bruch, Gai & Ingber (2022; TOIS), "An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval".** [arXiv:2210.11934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934) (abstract page verified); venue per S2: ACM TOIS. Citations: 145 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: a modern counterpoint — on hybrid lexical/semantic retrieval, RRF is *parameter-sensitive* (including its k), and a tuned convex combination (CC) of normalized scores beat RRF both in- and out-of-domain, needing only a handful of training examples to pick the single mixing weight.
  Relevance: the honest caveat for our fixed k=60 unweighted RRF: a per-archive tuned weight (or at least a config-exposed k) is a measurable upgrade path — but only after an eval harness exists (recommendation 1), otherwise tuning is unmeasurable.

## 3. Hybrid sparse + dense retrieval

Positions what the two-leg architecture buys, and how strong the lexical leg alone is.

- **Karpukhin et al. (2020), "Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering", EMNLP 2020.** [arXiv:2004.04906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906) (verified). Citations: 7,893 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: a simple dual-encoder (question encoder + passage encoder, dot product) trained on a small QA set beat a strong Lucene BM25 baseline by 9–19% top-20 accuracy — the paper that made dense retrieval a standard leg.
  Relevance: the argument for mailchat-vault's vector leg in principle; also a reminder that the *training signal* is what dense legs need, which a personal archive lacks — supporting the current choice of a cheap deterministic embedder plus fusion, rather than a randomly-initialized encoder.

- **Khattab & Zaharia (2020), "ColBERT: Efficient and Effective Passage Search via Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT", SIGIR 2020.** [arXiv:2004.12832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832) (verified). Citations: 2,872 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: encode query and document separately but keep *all* token vectors, and score with cheap MaxSim late interaction — near-cross-encoder quality at ~2 orders of magnitude lower query-time cost, because document representations are precomputed.
  Relevance: the reference point for "if we ever bundle a real model" — late interaction over stored token vectors trades storage (~100× document size) for quality; at 100k–1M messages that is a multi-GB commitment, so it defines the expensive end of the design space rather than a near-term option.

- **Formal, Piwowarski & Clinchant (2021), "SPLADE: Sparse Lexical and Expansion Model for First Stage Ranking", SIGIR 2021.** [arXiv:2107.05720](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05720) (verified). Citations: 804 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: a neural model that learns *term expansion* into a sparse inverted-index-compatible vector — strong evidence that expansion (answering synonyms/related terms the query never says) is the biggest single lexical-side quality lever.
  Relevance: we will not ship a transformer, but SPLADE's finding justifies non-neural expansion: local PRF (Rocchio) or consent-gated LLM expansion (Query2doc) attack the same vocabulary-mismatch problem with zero model weight.

- **Thakur et al. (2021), "BEIR: A Heterogenous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models", NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks.** [arXiv:2104.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663) (verified; note "Heterogenous" is the original title's spelling). Citations: 2,300 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: across 18 diverse tasks, BM25 remained a strong baseline that many dense systems failed to beat out-of-domain; systems that dominate one benchmark often generalize poorly. Methodologically, it is the reference for building a *heterogeneous* retrieval eval before tuning.
  Relevance: two lessons — (1) keep the FTS5 leg strong and don't assume the vector leg should dominate fusion; (2) our eval harness should cover several query families (names, subjects, dates, paraphrases, chat slang), not one, before any fusion-weight tuning is trusted.

## 4. Hashing, MinHash/SimHash, and lightweight embeddings

The ancestry of `hash-ngram-v1`, and the model it could become.

- **Weinberger, Dasgupta, Langford, Smola & Attenberg (2009), "Feature Hashing for Large Scale Multitask Learning", ICML 2009.** [DOI 10.1145/1553374.1553516](https://doi.org/10.1145/1553374.1553516); [author-hosted PDF](https://alex.smola.org/papers/2009/Weinbergeretal09.pdf) (verified). Citations: 1,151 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: hashing high-dimensional features into a fixed number of signed buckets (the "hashing trick", with a second hash determining sign, exactly as `HashEmbedder` does with FNV-1a) is a bias-free-enough dimensionality reduction whose collision noise costs little accuracy at large scale.
  Relevance: the primary citation for the shipped embedder. The paper's collision analysis supports our empirical MIN_COSINE=0.08 floor (unrelated pairs ≲0.06 at 512 buckets) and suggests 512→1024 buckets would halve collision noise cheaply if precision matters more than 1 KB/message storage.

- **Charikar (2002), "Similarity Estimation Techniques from Rounding Algorithms", STOC 2002.** [DOI 10.1145/509907.509965](https://doi.org/10.1145/509907.509965); author-page PDF (verified). Citations: 2,887 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: SimHash — random hyperplane rounding producing fingerprints whose Hamming distance approximates cosine similarity; estimation quality follows the Chernoff-style bounds of the rounding scheme.
  Relevance: a 64-bit SimHash per message is 8 bytes (vs 512) and enables near-duplicate *pre-filtering* via Hamming-distance buckets in SQLite — a candidate cheap pre-pass for the revision-identity and accidental-double-import problems.

- **Broder (1997), "On the Resemblance and Containment of Complete Documents", STOC 1997** (indexed by S2/IEEE under the 1997 Sequences-workshop printing). [DOI 10.1109/sequen.1997.666900](https://doi.org/10.1109/sequen.1997.666900); the companion systems paper is "Syntactic Clustering of the Web" ([DOI 10.1016/s0169-7552(97)00031-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0169-7552(97)00031-7), 1,575 citations, Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13). Citations: 2,534 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: shingling + MinHash + Jaccard containment — the vocabulary of near-duplicate detection; resemblance is estimable from a small sketch regardless of document size.
  Relevance: grounds the content-derived chat-identity keys and the "semantic revision hash" idea: a MinHash sketch of normalized text tolerates quoting/mangling that exact SHA-256 identity does not, at ~100 bytes per message.

- **Manku, Jain & Das Sarma (2007), "Detecting Near-Duplicates for Web Crawling", WWW 2007.** [DOI 10.1145/1242572.1242592](https://doi.org/10.1145/1242572.1242592); [Google research-hosted PDF](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/33026.pdf) (verified). Citations: 747 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the engineering sequel — SimHash fingerprints + a cleverly-hammered 64-bit fingerprint + table-based lookup found near-duplicates in a multi-billion-page crawl; the pragmatics of doing Broder/Charikar at scale.
  Relevance: proof that fingerprint-based near-dup detection works with tiny memory and simple tables — i.e., that mailchat-vault's dedup ambitions do not need any indexing structure beyond SQLite tables.

- **Wang, Zhang, Song, Sebe & Shen (2016/2018), "A Survey on Learning to Hash", IEEE TPAMI.** [arXiv:1606.00185](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.00185) (verified). Citations: 1,114 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: taxonomy of learning-to-hash methods; the survey's conclusion worth quoting is that quantization-style methods dominate on accuracy/time/space trade-offs versus binary hashing — i.e., i8-quantized float vectors (what we store) sit in the better regime than 1-bit hashes.
  Relevance: validates the design choice of a 512-byte i8 vector over a 512-bit binary hash: we keep cosine's graded similarity (needed for ranking) rather than a binary filter.

- **Reimers & Gurevych (2019), "Sentence-BERT: Sentence Embeddings using Siamese BERT-Networks", EMNLP 2019.** [arXiv:1908.10084](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084) (verified). Citations: 22,440 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: siamese fine-tuning produces sentence embeddings that are directly comparable by cosine — cutting 10k-sentence similarity search from 65 hours (cross-encoder) to ~5 seconds; the foundation of the MiniLM/all-mpnet family used everywhere since.
  Relevance: the target class for the future drop-in behind the `Embedder` trait: an ONNX-quantized MiniLM-class model (e.g., 384-dim MiniLM, ~90 MB fp32 / ~23 MB int8) would make the vector leg semantically real. The tradeoff is explicit: a multi-MB binary bundled (or shipped as an optional build-time artifact) versus the current zero-weight model; the model-id-versioned embedding rows already make the migration a re-embed job.

## 5. Vector search engineering

When linear scan stops being right, and what to do then.

- **Jégou, Douze & Schmid (2011), "Product Quantization for Nearest Neighbor Search", IEEE TPAMI 33(1):116–128.** [DOI 10.1109/tpami.2010.57](https://doi.org/10.1109/tpami.2010.57). Citations: 3,992 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: split vectors into subvectors, quantize each with a small codebook (typically 8 subspaces × 8 bits = 64 bits/vector), and search via asymmetric distance computation — 10–30× memory reduction at modest recall loss; the canonical ANN-era compression.
  Relevance: at 1M messages our vectors alone are ~512 MB; PQ codes would be 8 MB (with the original i8 blobs demoted to a re-rank set). The threshold logic to write down: linear scan stays right while p95 scan time is within the Ask latency budget (~75 ms/100k today), and PQ (not HNSW) is the first step past it because it keeps the simple scan codepath.

- **Malkov & Yashunin (2016/2018), "Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs", IEEE TPAMI.** [arXiv:1603.09320](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320) (verified). Citations: 2,971 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: multi-layer proximity graphs give logarithmic ANN search with excellent recall on clustered real data — the algorithm behind hnswlib, FAISS's HNSW, sqlite-vec's successor designs, and usearch.
  Relevance: the known next step if archives grow past a few million messages or the embedder moves to higher dimensions; the cost is index build time, extra dependencies (sqlite-vec/usearch), and tuning — deliberately out of scope at personal-archive scale.

- **Guo et al. (2020), "Accelerating Large-Scale Inference with Anisotropic Vector Quantization", ICML 2020 (ScaNN).** [arXiv:1908.10396](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10396) (verified; original arXiv v1 was titled "New Loss Functions for Fast Maximum Inner Product Search"). Citations: 646 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: quantize with a loss function that weights residual error by its effect on inner products with queries (penalize the parallel component more than the orthogonal one) — better recall at the same code size than reconstruction-loss PQ.
  Relevance: the advanced refinement if/when quantization is adopted; its scalar-quantizer variant is close to what our i8 already does, and the anisotropic insight could shape a future codebook. Strategic, not near-term.

## 6. RAG quality

The Ask pipeline (translate → retrieve → summarize top-8 with [n] citations) sits squarely in this literature.

- **Lewis et al. (2020), "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks", NeurIPS 2020.** [arXiv:2005.11401](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401) (verified). Citations: 21,049 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the original RAG — parametric memory plus a non-parametric retriever produced more specific, diverse, and factual outputs than a closed-book model, with the generator able to update knowledge without retraining.
  Relevance: the design precedent for keeping the LLM *outside* the archive: our generator only ever sees 8 consented rows. The paper's "knowledge can change without retraining" maps to re-imports/updates changing answers with zero model changes.

- **Liu et al. (2023), "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts", TACL 2023.** [arXiv:2307.03172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172) (verified). Citations: 6,006 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: in multi-document QA, models answer best when the relevant document is at the *beginning or end* of the prompt and degrade substantially when it is in the middle — a positional bias present in both closed and open models.
  Relevance: directly actionable in the summarize step's context builder: order the 8 blocks so the strongest fused hits are first *and* last (e.g., ranks 1,3,5,7 then 8,6,4,2), rather than feeding them in rank order. Trivial code, measurable gain.

- **Gao, Yen, Yu & Chen (2023), "Enabling Large Language Models to Generate Text with Citations", EMNLP 2023.** [arXiv:2305.14627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627) (verified). Citations: 974 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the ALCE benchmark shows even top models produce outputs "lacking complete citation support ~50% of the time" on ELI5, and formalizes citation recall/precision metrics — is each sentence supported by the passages it cites, and are citations used at all.
  Relevance: our [1][2] citations need a faithfulness check, not just rendering. A local post-check (every answer sentence must lexically overlap its cited row's text; every citation index must resolve to a real row) is implementable without any model; a stronger NLI-based check would need a bundled model (strategic).

- **Liu, Zhang & Liang (2023), "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines", Findings of EMNLP 2023.** [arXiv:2304.09848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848) (verified). Citations: 513 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: human audit of Bing Chat/Neeva/Perplexity/YouChat found only 51.5% of generated sentences fully supported by their citations, and 74.5% of citations actually supported their sentence — for systems that *look* trustworthy.
  Relevance: the independent evidence that citation UI alone does not equal verifiability; motivates both the local post-check above and the UX rule that every citation opens the exact archived message (already implemented) — the check closes the loop.

- **Asai et al. (2023/2024), "Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique through Self-Reflection", ICLR 2024.** [arXiv:2310.11511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11511) (verified). Citations: 3,119 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: a generator trained to emit reflection tokens decides *when* to retrieve and critiques its own drafts and their support, improving factuality and citation accuracy versus always-retrieve RAG.
  Relevance: the transferable idea (not the trained tokens) is a second, cheap verification pass: after summarization, one more consent-gated call asks "is each cited sentence supported by row n?" — gating output on the answer. Fits the consent architecture since it reuses the same bounded payload.

- **Es, James, Espinosa-Anke & Schockaert (2023/2024), "RAGAS: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation", EACL 2024 (demo).** [arXiv:2309.15217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217) (verified). Citations: 1,373 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: reference-free RAG metrics — faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision/recall — computed by prompting an LLM judge, so pipelines can be evaluated without human labels.
  Relevance: the methodology for a periodic Ask-quality self-test; note the constraint tension — RAGAS needs an LLM judge, so in mailchat-vault it can only run inside a consented session (fine for developer testing and opt-in user diagnostics, never as background telemetry).

- **Sarthi et al. (2024), "RAPTOR: Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval", ICLR 2024.** [arXiv:2401.18059](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18059) (verified). Citations: 877 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: recursively cluster-and-summarize a corpus into a tree and retrieve over all abstraction levels; large gains on questions needing synthesis across many chunks (e.g., +20% on QuALITY with GPT-4).
  Relevance: the principled version of "thread summaries": precompute per-thread/per-conversation summaries (local storage, incremental on import) and let Ask retrieve summaries *and* messages — answering "what did we decide about X" over long threads without ballooning the 500-char truncations. The pattern's simpler cousin — retrieve small (message), feed big (thread context) — needs no summaries at all.

## 7. Query understanding

The Ask translate step is natural-language → FTS5 MATCH; this is the neighboring evidence.

- **Wang, Yang & Wei (2023), "Query2doc: Query Expansion with Large Language Models", EMNLP 2023.** [arXiv:2303.07678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07678) (verified). Citations: 343 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: prompting an LLM to write a small pseudo-document from the query, then appending it to the query, lifted BM25 by 3–15% on MS-MARCO/TREC-DL with no retrieval-side training — expansion fixes vocabulary mismatch.
  Relevance: composable with the existing translate step at almost zero cost: when the LLM is already consented and translating the question, ask it for 5–10 expansion terms in the same JSON response and OR them into the MATCH expression (with the plain-term fallback unchanged if it misbehaves). This is the consent-compatible half of SPLADE's win.

## 8. Email threading algorithms

The one-hop In-Reply-To resolution is a simplification of a well-documented lineage.

- **Zawinski (1997–2002), "Message Threading", jwz.org web document.** [https://www.jwz.org/doc/threading.html](https://www.jwz.org/doc/threading.html) (verified, primary source). Citations: not indexed (Semantic Scholar does not index the web document).
  Takeaway: the five-step JWZ algorithm — build containers by Message-ID link, keep existing links, refuse loop-creating links, prune empty containers by promoting children, then *group the root set by normalized subject* — is "incredibly robust in the face of garbage input", threads 100k messages in memory in well under a second, and explicitly assumes References headers will be truncated and contradictory.
  Relevance: the two features our one-hop resolution lacks are dummy-container promotion and subject-based root grouping — both are local, incremental-friendly, and specifically designed for incomplete chains (deleted/unsent parents), which mbox and chat exports routinely have. This is the reference algorithm if threading complaints appear.

- **Crispin & Murchison (2008), RFC 5256, "Internet Message Access Protocol - SORT and THREAD Extensions", IETF.** [https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5256.html](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5256.html) (verified). Citations: not indexed (Semantic Scholar does not index RFCs).
  Takeaway: formalizes JWZ as the IMAP THREAD=REFERENCES algorithm (six steps: link by References/In-Reply-To, avoid loops, promote children of dummies, sort siblings by date, merge same-subject threads) plus a cheap ORDEREDSUBJECT fallback; it notes the algorithm descends from Netscape Mail 2.0/3.0.
  Relevance: the interop standard other mail software implements; matching its REFERENCES semantics (rather than inventing threading rules) keeps imports/export and mental models aligned. Its subject-merge step is the same gap JWZ step 5 covers.

- **Lewis & Knowles (1997), "Threading Electronic Mail — A Preliminary Study", Information Processing & Management 33(2):209–217.** [DOI 10.1016/S0306-4573(96)00063-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4573(96)00063-5). *Seed-list correction: the venue is IP&M, not CHI 1997.* Citations: 178 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: an early empirical study of threading — header-based linking is fragile on real mail (missing/mangled In-Reply-To), so it explores content-based similarity as a supplement; documents that pure header threading leaves many orphans even in 1997 corpora.
  Relevance: the historical justification for the repo's content-derived keys for chat conversations (where no headers exist) and for subject-based fallback grouping for mail.

- **Ailon, Karnin, Liberty & Maarek (2013), "Threading Machine Generated Email", WSDM 2013, pp. 405–414.** [DOI 10.1145/2433396.2433447](https://doi.org/10.1145/2433396.2433447) (bibliographic record verified via S2/ACM). Citations: 43 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: threading machine-generated mail (notifications, receipts, newsletters) with learned features; documents the modern reality that a growing share of mail has clean headers but *conversational* threading still misgroups — and that simple heuristics remain competitive.
  Relevance: reassurance that the roadmap does *not* need ML threading; one-hop + subject grouping covers the practical cases at personal scale.

## 9. Personal information management and re-finding

The oldest and most human literature here; it says what a personal archive is *for*.

- **Dumais et al. (2003), "Stuff I've Seen: A System for Personal Information Retrieval and Re-Use", SIGIR 2003.** [DOI 10.1145/860435.860451](https://doi.org/10.1145/860435.860451); [SIGIR Forum Test-of-Time reprint PDF](https://sigir.org/files/forum/2015D/p028.pdf) (verified). Citations: 621 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: unified index over everything a person has seen (mail, files, web, calendar) with rich contextual cues; deployed to 230+ users, it found "time and people are important retrieval cues" and users abandoned other search tools — re-finding is the dominant use of a personal archive.
  Relevance: the product's founding citation. Concretely: date and correspondent should be first-class in ranking and UI (they are in the data model already); recency/period facets and per-correspondent filters are the two features SIS says matter most. SIS also validates indexing seen-items over organizing them — exactly mailchat-vault's archive-then-search stance.

- **Gemmell, Bell, Lueder, Drucker & Wong (2002), "MyLifeBits: Fulfilling the Memex Vision", ACM Multimedia 2002.** [DOI 10.1145/641007.641053](https://doi.org/10.1145/641007.641053); [Microsoft Research-hosted PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2002/12/mylifebitsmm02.pdf) (verified). Citations: 611 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the lifetime-store argument — for a personal archive, "collections and search must replace hierarchy for organization"; annotations become critical for non-text media; structured storage with hyperlinks beats folder trees.
  Relevance: the lineage (Bush's Memex → MyLifeBits → local-first tools) mailchat-vault belongs to; also early evidence for two roadmap items — annotations as first-class archive data, and search-over-hierarchy (which the app already does by design).

- **Whittaker & Sidner (1996), "Email Overload: Exploring Personal Information Management of Email", CHI 1996, pp. 276–283.** [DOI 10.1145/238386.238530](https://doi.org/10.1145/238386.238530). Citations: 1,167 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the classic field study of email overload — users keep pending tasks in overflowing inboxes; filing behavior splits into no filers / spring cleaners / frequent filers, and *none* of the filing strategies kept up; email is task management as much as communication.
  Relevance: explains why users arrive with 100k+ unorganized messages and why folder-first import (mbox) plus search is the right primary UX; supports "search-first, folders optional" in the roadmap.

- **Whittaker, Matthews, Cerruti, Badenes & Tang (2011), "Am I Wasting My Time Organizing Email? A Study of Email Refinding", CHI 2011.** [DOI 10.1145/1978942.1979457](https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979457). Citations: 127 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: instrumented 345 users (~85,000 refinding actions): elaborate folder structures did not improve retrieval success, and search was faster than folder navigation — the title's answer is essentially "yes".
  Relevance: the empirical license to spend engineering effort on ranking quality rather than any folder-management features; also implies the import UX should never demand curation before the archive is useful.

- **Teevan, Alvarado, Ackerman & Karger (2004), "The Perfect Search Engine Is Not Enough: A Study of Orienteering Behavior in Directed Search", CHI 2004, pp. 415–422.** [DOI 10.1145/985692.985745](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985745). *Seed-list correction: authors are Teevan/Alvarado/Ackerman/Karger (not Jones/Bederson).* Citations: 707 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: people deliberately search *incrementally* — starting from familiar landmarks (a person, a date, a thread) and stepping closer — rather than formulating one perfect query, even when search is excellent.
  Relevance: UX guidance: results should expose and preserve landmarks (correspondent, date, thread, source) as clickable refinements — turning each result row into the next query step. Cheap UI work with outsized re-finding value.

- **Teevan, Adar, Jones & Potts (2007), "Information Re-retrieval: Repeat Queries in Yahoo's Logs", SIGIR 2007, pp. 151–158.** [DOI 10.1145/1277741.1277770](https://doi.org/10.1145/1277741.1277770). Citations: 376 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: a large fraction of queries are re-finding (repeat queries), re-finding queries are better predicted by *the user's own history* than by global statistics, and what was clicked before predicts what will be clicked again.
  Relevance: the archive already knows what the user opened; storing per-user result-opens (local only) enables boosting previously-clicked messages for repeated queries — a personalization signal with zero privacy cost, entirely in SQLite.

## 10. Near-duplicate detection and content-defined identity

The SHA-256 media store and revision identity sit on established ground.

- **Xia et al. (2016), "FastCDC: A Fast and Efficient Content-Defined Chunking Approach for Data Deduplication", USENIX ATC 2016, pp. 101–114.** [USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc16/technical-sessions/presentation/xia) (verified). *Seed-list correction: FastCDC appeared at ATC '16; the FAST '16 paper by the same group is the survey below.* Citations: 170 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: content-defined chunking (Cut-point detection via a Gear rolling hash, skipping, normalized chunk sizes) makes deduplication robust to inserts/shifts at ~10× the speed of Rabin CDC — the state of the art for byte-stream chunking.
  Relevance: relevant only if attachment dedup granularity matters later (whole-object SHA-256 already collapses exact duplicates); CDC would additionally dedupe re-sent documents with small edits (signatures, timestamps). Deferred-workshelf item, grounded.

- **Xia, Jiang, Feng, Douglis, Shilane et al. (2016), "A Comprehensive Study of the Past, Present, and Future of Data Deduplication", FAST 2016 / Proceedings of the IEEE 104(9):1681–1710.** [DOI 10.1109/jproc.2016.2571298](https://doi.org/10.1109/jproc.2016.2571298) (FAST '16 + journal record verified via DOI/S2). Citations: 330 (Semantic Scholar, 2026-11-13).
  Takeaway: the field survey — dedup literature converged on content-defined chunking + fingerprint indexing + post-dedup layout; also catalogs identity and consistency pitfalls.
  Relevance: confirms the current design (content-addressed blobs, idempotent re-import) matches standard practice for personal-scale stores, and is the map to consult if dedup expands to attachments (bucketed fingerprint index, backup-aware layout).

## Ranked recommendations — what we could add

Priority order; each item names the grounding papers, constraint fit, rough effort (S < a day-ish / M < a week-ish / L larger), risk notes, and whether it is a cheap win or a strategic bet.

1. **Build the retrieval eval harness before any tuning (cheap win, S→M).** What: a small labeled query set (subject-line queries, name queries, date+term, paraphrases, chat slang) over the synthetic fixture archives, scored with recall@k and nDCG for the FTS5 leg, the vector leg, and fused output; wire into CI like `tests/vector_perf.rs`. Grounded in: Thakur et al. 2021 (BEIR methodology — evaluate heterogeneous query families before trusting any single number); Es et al. 2023 (RAG-level metrics, consent-constrained — see item 6). Why it fits: fully local fixtures, no model, no network; makes every item below measurable. Risk: none. Everything else on this list is blind without it.
2. **Expose and weight the `subject` column BM25F-style (cheap win, S→M).** *Corrected against the code 2026-11-13 after review:* the canonical `archive_fts` **already indexes** `(body, author, subject)` (`crates/vault-core/src/storage.rs`, trigger-synced); it was the legacy v1–2 `messages_fts` that lacked `subject`, and email bodies additionally carry the subject as a text prefix for FTS (`crates/vault-core/src/mail.rs` — `format!("{subject}\n\n{text}")`). The actual gaps: (a) the Ask translation prompt describes the index as two columns and allows only `body:`/`author:` filters (`crates/vault-core/src/llm.rs`), so LLM query plans can never target `subject:` explicitly (unfiltered terms still match it); (b) ranking is plain `ORDER BY rank` — default bm25() with equal column weights; `bm25()` with per-column weights appears nowhere, so a subject hit is not boosted over an incidental body hit, and for email the subject-in-body prefix double-counts subject terms. Do: allow `subject:` in the prompt and the MATCH validator; switch ranking to `bm25(archive_fts, w_body, w_author, w_subject)` with weights tuned on the eval harness; decide whether the email subject prefix stays once real weighting exists. Grounded in: Robertson, Zaragoza & Taylor 2004; Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 (BM25F: term evidence is field-dependent; title/subject carries the most). Why it fits: a SQL expression + prompt change, offline, no reindex (the columns already exist). Risk: verify the prompt change doesn't regress MATCH validation (existing fallback covers it); tune weights only against item 1's harness.
3. **Order the Ask context best-first *and* best-last (cheap win, S).** What: in the summarize step's context builder, order the 8 rows so the top-fused documents sit at the beginning and the runner-up at the end (e.g., 1,3,5,7,8,6,4,2), instead of strict rank order. Grounded in: Liu et al. 2023 (Lost in the Middle — positional degradation is largest mid-context). Why it fits: a dozen lines, no new data leaves the machine, no dependency. Risk: minimal; A/B on the eval harness.
4. **Recency- and history-aware ranking (cheap win, S→M).** What: (a) optional time-decay multiplier on the fused score with a UI toggle ("recent first" bias), (b) record result-opens locally and boost previously-opened messages for similar queries. Grounded in: Dumais et al. 2003 (time and people are the dominant re-finding cues); Teevan et al. 2007 (re-finding is common and best predicted by personal history); Whittaker et al. 2011 (search-first refinding is the winning behavior — the ranking should serve it). Why it fits: pure local signals (SQLite tables already know sent_at and opens); no telemetry leaves the machine ever. Risk: keep the decay off by default or personal archives stop being neutral; opens-boost needs cold-start care.
5. **Local pseudo-relevance feedback plus consent-gated LLM expansion (M).** What: (a) after a weak FTS result set, take discriminating terms from the top few hits and OR-expand the MATCH (Rocchio-style, fully local); (b) in the Ask translate step, request expansion terms in the same JSON response and merge them into the MATCH (fallback path unchanged). Grounded in: Rocchio 1971 (PRF); Wang et al. 2023 (Query2doc — 3–15% BM25 lift from LLM pseudo-documents/expansion, no retrieval retraining); Formal et al. 2021 (SPLADE — the vocabulary-mismatch problem expansion solves). Why it fits: (a) is zero-dependency offline; (b) reuses the already-consented, already-bounded payload. Risk: expansion can drift on short/ambiguous queries — keep it behind the existing failure fallback and evaluate with item 1.
6. **Citation-faithfulness checks on Ask output (cheap win → strategic, S for local check, M for LLM/NLI judge).** What: (a) local structural check — every [n] resolves to a real row and each answer sentence shares lexical overlap with its cited row's text (refuse or annotate otherwise); (b) optional consented second LLM pass that verifies support sentence-by-sentence (Self-RAG-style critique). Grounded in: Gao et al. 2023 (ALCE: ~50% of sentences lack complete citation support in strong models; citation recall/precision metrics); Liu, Zhang & Liang 2023 (51.5% of commercial generative-search sentences fully supported); Asai et al. 2023 (critique pass improves factuality). Why it fits: (a) is pure local code; (b) stays inside the session-scoped consent gate with the same 8-row payload. Risk: lexical-overlap checks have false negatives (paraphrase); state that in UX copy rather than silently failing answers.
7. **Small-to-big retrieval: retrieve messages, feed thread context (M).** What: when a summary cites a message, feed the summarizer the surrounding thread window (±N messages or a character budget) for that row, keeping the top-8 selection unchanged; optionally precompute per-thread summaries incrementally at import (RAPTOR-lite) and index them like messages. Grounded in: Sarthi et al. 2024 (retrieval over multiple granularities/summaries helps synthesis questions); the small-to-big/parent-document pattern it formalizes. Why it fits: all local; bounded payload growth stays consent-visible (say: up to 2 KB per cited thread); thread summaries are cheap SQLite rows that make "what did we decide" questions answerable. Risk: payload size disclosure in consent copy must grow; summary staleness on re-import (recompute per thread, incremental by design).
8. **Tunable fusion: config-exposed weights or convex combination, evaluated against plain RRF (M).** What: keep RRF k=60 as default; add an optional weight on the vector leg's RRF term (weighted RRF) or a normalized-score convex combination, chosen per-archive by the eval harness on fixtures; expose k as a setting. Grounded in: Cormack et al. 2009 (k=60 was near-optimal in their pilot; unweighted RRF is training-free); Bruch et al. 2022 (RRF is parameter-sensitive; a tuned convex combination beat RRF with few examples). Why it fits: deterministic, offline, per-archive tuning on the user's own fixtures avoids cloud training. Risk: overfitting tiny eval sets — require the harness (item 1) and keep unweighted RRF as the shipped default.
9. **Bundled real-embedding upgrade behind the `Embedder` trait (strategic bet, M→L).** What: ship an optional ONNX int8-quantized MiniLM/SBERT-class model (384-dim, on the order of 20–25 MB quantized) as a build-time artifact — never a runtime download — selected by a new model id so existing rows re-embed via the existing incremental job; keep `hash-ngram-v1` as the zero-download default. Grounded in: Reimers & Gurevych 2019 (SBERT-class embeddings, cosine-comparable); Karpukhin et al. 2020 (dense retrieval's win comes from trained encoders — which a hash embedder cannot capture); Wang et al. 2018 (quantized float vectors sit in the right accuracy/space regime vs binary hashes). Why it fits: the trait plus model-id-versioned rows were built for exactly this; the offline promise is preserved because the blob ships with the installer (state the size in release notes and settings copy). Risk/dependency: ONNX runtime is a native build dependency previously ruled out for v1 — that cost is the decision; scan cost at 384-dim is lower than today's 512; embedding quality should be proven on the eval harness first.
10. **Write down the quantization/ANN thresholds; only implement past them (engineering, S to codify, M to implement).** What: document the decision rule — linear scan stays while p95 vector scan ≤ the Ask budget (measured ~75 ms/100k; 1M ≈ 0.75 s and ~512 MB of blobs), then adopt product quantization codes with i8 re-rank first, HNSW (sqlite-vec/usearch) only if dimensions or corpus grow past PQ's comfort. Grounded in: Jégou et al. 2011 (PQ: ~8× memory cut at modest recall loss); Malkov & Yashunin 2018 (HNSW for when graph indexes are justified); Guo et al. 2020 (anisotropic quantization as the refined variant). Why it fits: avoids premature dependencies; both thresholds live behind existing perf tests. Risk: PQ recall loss needs measurement on the eval harness before shipping any switch.
11. **Threading: subject-based root grouping and dummy-container promotion (cheap win, S→M).** What: extend the one-hop In-Reply-To resolution with (a) creating placeholder parents when multiple messages reference a missing message-id, promoting children when the parent is absent (RFC 5256 semantics), and (b) grouping unlinked messages that share a normalized `Re:`-stripped subject into one thread. Grounded in: Zawinski 1997–2002 (steps 4–5 of the algorithm, robustness argument, 100k-message performance); Crispin & Murchison 2008 (RFC 5256 REFERENCES — the interop reference); Lewis & Knowles 1997 (header fragility on real mail); Ailon et al. 2013 (heuristic threading remains competitive with ML — don't over-build). Why it fits: pure local graph work over existing headers; incremental on import; explicitly safe for cycles/incomplete chains by construction. Risk: subject grouping over-merges on generic subjects ("Re: hi") — JWZ mitigates via container ordering; keep it toggleable.
12. **Near-duplicate fingerprints for revision identity (deferred, S to spike).** What: optional 64-bit SimHash or MinHash sketch per message body (stored beside the revision hash) to catch near-duplicate messages across re-imports/sources and accidental double-imports beyond exact SHA-256 identity. Grounded in: Charikar 2002 (SimHash); Broder 1997 (resemblance/containment); Manku et al. 2007 (fingerprint lookup at scale is easy). Why it fits: 8–100 bytes/message, local tables, no dependencies. Risk: only worth it if duplicates are an observed complaint — collect evidence from the failed-item ledger before building.

Explicitly *not* recommended by the evidence: ML-based threading (Ailon et al. — heuristics competitive), folder-first organization features (Whittaker et al. 2011; Gemmell et al. 2002), late-interaction token-vector indexes at this scale (Khattab & Zaharia — storage cost), and any runtime model download or cloud-side ranking telemetry (violates the offline/consent constraints; every recommendation above preserves them).
